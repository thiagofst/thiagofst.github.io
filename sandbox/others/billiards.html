Model and integration

The table is treated as a plane region bounded by a closed outline, which is a rectangle of by units on the classic table and one of eleven other outlines in the Shapes menu, all of them non-convex: the T, the star, the cross, the peanut, the banana, the cluster of grapes, the doughnut, the lightning bolt, the Pac-Man, the outline of Brazil (digitised from Natural Earth 110 m country boundaries) and a giraffe, of which the doughnut is an annulus with a hole at its centre, together with any simple polygon that the player draws in the separate Make your own editor, and on it every ball is a disc of radius R = , so that the game reduces to the motion of sixteen centres subject to friction, to collisions between discs and to reflections at the cushions. Positions are advanced with an explicit Euler scheme in steps of dt = 1/ s,

r ← r + v dt(1)

which is crude in the abstract but sufficient here, because the fastest ball, with a speed of at most Vmax = units per second, moves by only about units per step, far less than a ball diameter, and cannot therefore pass through another ball or a cushion between two consecutive checks. A shot of relative power p, which the player sets through the length of the pull-back and which is restricted to the interval [, 1], launches the cue ball with the speed

v0 = p Vmax.(2)

Motion on the cloth

Besides the position r and the velocity v of its centre, each ball carries an angular velocity ω = (ωx, ωy, ωz), in which (ωx, ωy) describes top spin, back spin and roll and ωz is the side spin about the vertical axis. A ball of unit mass has the moment of inertia I = (2/5)R2 and touches the cloth at a point a distance R below its centre, which moves relative to the cloth with the velocity

u = (vx − R ωy, vy + R ωx),(3)

so that u = 0 is the condition for rolling without slipping. Whilst |u| exceeds units per second the contact slides, and the cloth exerts a friction of constant magnitude as = units per second squared directed against u, which changes the velocity and the spin together,

dv/dt = −as û, dωx/dt = −(5as/2R) ûy, dωy/dt = (5as/2R) ûx,(4)

where û = u/|u| and the factor 5/2 is R2/I. Because translation and rotation are both altered, |u| falls at the rate (7/2)as, and the slide lasts

ts = 2|u0| / (7as),(5)

after which a ball that began without spin retains 5/7 of its initial speed. The same law accounts for draw and follow without any further rule: a ball with back spin has u pointing backwards, so that the cloth accelerates it backwards until it rolls, whereas a ball with top spin is accelerated forwards. Once u vanishes the ball rolls, with ωx = −vy/R and ωy = vx/R, and its speed s = |v| decays through a drag proportional to the speed, standing for the combined effect of cloth and air, and a constant deceleration, standing for rolling resistance, which dominates at low speed,

ds/dt = −K s − A, K = s−1, A = units s−2,(6)

of which the game applies the discrete counterpart s ← s e−K dt − A dt at every step, declaring a ball to be at rest once s falls below units per second. Equation (6) integrates to

s(t) = (s0 + A/K) e−K t − A/K,(7)

which vanishes at t* = K−1 ln(1 + K s0/A), and a further integration over this interval gives the distance a rolling ball covers before it stops,

d(s0) = s0/K − (A/K2) ln(1 + K s0/A).(8)

A ball that rolls at the highest speed therefore travels about units, roughly table lengths if no ball or cushion intervenes, whereas a ball launched without spin first loses the fraction 2/7 of its speed in the slide, so that equation (8) overestimates its travel; the computer opponent nevertheless inverts it numerically as a first estimate of the speed that leaves a ball at a chosen distance and corrects the choice by simulation. Side spin is removed by the pivoting of the ball on the cloth, which is represented by ωz ← ωz e−ζ dt with ζ = s−1.

The cue strike

The small disc beside the toolbar selects the point at which the cue strikes the cue ball. Its coordinates (u, w), with u to the right and w downwards, are converted into a follow parameter σf = −w, positive for a strike above the centre, and a side parameter σs = u, each limited to a magnitude of 0.85. The cue is inclined at ε = ° to the table, so that, with d the unit vector along the shot,

The first term is top or back spin about the horizontal axis perpendicular to the shot and the second is side spin, whose small horizontal component along the line of the shot gives the cloth a sideways slip through equation (3) whilst the ball slides, so that the ball curves towards the side on which it was struck (swerve). The ball moreover leaves the cue not along d but turned slightly away from the side of the strike (squirt), and with r̂ = (−dy, dx) the unit vector to the right of the shot,

which is a deflection of ° at the largest side offset. The ball keeps its spin until it meets another ball or a cushion, and what it does afterwards follows from equation (4) alone, so that no separate damping of follow or draw with distance is needed.

Collisions between balls

Two balls collide when the distance between their centres drops below 2R. Writing n for the unit vector from ball a to ball b and rel for the component of the relative velocity along it,

n = (rb − ra) / |rb − ra|, rel = (va − vb) · n,(11)

a contact is processed only if rel > 0, that is, whilst the balls are still approaching one another. The masses being equal, the exchange along n takes place with the impulse

J = (1 + eB) rel / 2, va′ = va − J n, vb′ = vb + J n, eB = − rel/Vmax,(12)

where the coefficient of restitution eB falls from for gentle contacts to at the highest closing speed. Momentum is conserved and the relative normal velocity is reversed and scaled by eB, so that a head-on shot almost stops the cue ball and a cut shot sends the two balls apart at nearly a right angle. Friction between the surfaces then acts along the tangent t = (−ny, nx). With the slip speed of the two contact points

st = (va − vb) · t + R (ωz,a + ωz,b), Jt = clamp(st/7, ±μB J), μB = ,(13)

the velocities change by ∓Jt t for balls a and b and both side spins by −5Jt/2R, the divisor 7 being the effective mass of the contact for two balls with the moment of inertia given above. A cut shot therefore throws the object ball slightly off the line of centres, in the direction in which the surface of the cue ball moves past it, and side spin on the cue ball is passed on to the object ball with the opposite sign. Any overlap left by the finite step is removed by moving each ball by half the penetration along n.

Cushions

Each cushion is a straight segment with a unit normal pointing into the table, and curved cushions, as on the peanut, the banana, the grapes, the doughnut and the Pac-Man, are approximated by many short segments. For the point c of a segment that lies nearest to the ball centre r, a contact occurs when

d = |r − c| < R, n = (r − c) / d,(14)

and the ball is then moved back to a distance R from the cushion along n. At a corner, c is the end point of the segment, so that the ball rolls around a rounded vertex instead of catching on it. The normal velocity vn < 0 is reversed with a coefficient that decreases with speed,

vn′ = −eW vn, eW = − |vn|/Vmax,(15)

which is about 0.73 at a normal speed of 600 units per second and 0.50 at the highest speed. The cushion nose touches the ball at a height R above its centre, that is at an angle φ = ° from the horizontal, so that a spinning ball does not slip along the cushion in the way it would at the equator. With Jn = (1 + eW)|vn|, the tangent t = (−ny, nx) and the component ωh of the horizontal spin along n, the slip and the friction impulse are

sw = v · t − R (cos φ ωz + sin φ ωh), Jw = clamp(−sw/(7/2), ±μW Jn), μW = ,(16)

and the ball receives the velocity change Jw t together with the spin changes Δωz = −5Jw cos φ/2R and Δωh = −5Jw sin φ/2R, the divisor 7/2 being 1 + R2/I. English therefore alters the rebound angle, a running spin is partly spent at each contact, and the tangential speed is reduced even for a ball without side spin. The impulse from the nose also has a vertical component on a real table, which is not represented, so that the ball stays on the cloth and top or back spin is unaffected by a perpendicular impact. To keep the test cheap, the segments are sorted into a grid of square cells of units and a ball is tested only against the segments registered in its own cell, balls at rest being skipped altogether.

Pockets

A ball is potted when its centre comes within a capture radius ρk of the centre pk of pocket k, provided that it is not moving too obliquely across the mouth,

|r − pk| < ρk, |v × (pk − r)| / |pk − r| < VJ, VJ = units s−1.(17)

The second condition bounds the component of the velocity across the line to the pocket centre, so that a ball travelling along that line drops at any speed whereas a fast ball that arrives at an angle is refused, meets the cushions beside the mouth, which act as the jaws, and may rattle in the opening or leave it.

On the classic table the six pockets sit at the corners of the playing area and, for the side pockets, six units beyond the middle of each long cushion, with capture radii of and units for the corner and side pockets. The other tables have pockets at their vertices and, on several of them, at the midpoints of some sides, as well as in the reflex corners, as at the waist of the peanut, in the notches of the cross and in the valleys between the grapes, whereas the star carries one at each of its five tips, the banana one at each end and four along its edges, and the doughnut six on its outer rim and three on the rim of its central hole, which is a closed cushion loop. A ball can approach a vertex of interior angle α only to within R/sin(α/2) of it, which is 38 units for the 47° tips of the star, and the capture radius of those five pockets is therefore raised to 46 units, whereas the 60° apex of the Pac-Man, where the bound is 30 units, carries a pocket of 36 units. The black openings drawn on each table are marginally smaller than the capture radii.

Setting up the balls

On every table other than the classic one the player positions the balls before the first shot, which is the only stage at which the motion is not governed by the equations above, although the placement is constrained by the same geometry. The cue ball may be dragged to any point that lies at least R from every cushion segment, that is at least 2 units beyond the capture radius of every pocket and that is at least 2R from every other ball, whereas the rack of fifteen balls, which on the built-in tables keeps its triangular packing, is moved as a rigid body and turned about its centroid, through any angle by dragging beside it, by 5° with the scroll wheel or by 60° with a tap. A displacement or rotation of the rack is accepted only if all fifteen centres still satisfy the cushion condition, remain at least R + 4 units outside the capture radius of every pocket and keep a distance of at least 2R + 1 from the cue ball, and a rejected displacement leaves the rack where it was, so that no starting configuration can violate the contact conditions of the simulation. On a table drawn by the player the balls may be arranged in another formation (see below), and in that case each ball is dragged on its own, subject to the cushion, pocket and contact conditions just given with a minimum separation of 2R + 0.6 from every other ball.

Tables drawn by the player

The outline of a table made by the player is drawn on a grid of units, shown as lines every units, in the same coordinates as the built-in tables, so that a ball of radius R can be compared directly with the drawing area of by units, and passages as narrow as a single ball, or tables barely larger than the balls, are allowed. The outline is accepted only if it is a simple polygon, in which no two non-adjacent edges intersect, with an area of at least 10 000 square units and at least one pocket, and the table is then refused unless fifteen balls and the cue ball can be placed on it. The cushion segments and their normals are generated in the same way as for the other tables. A pocket placed within units of a vertex is attached to it, with a capture radius equal to the larger of 32 units and R/sin(α/2) + 4 units at a convex vertex of interior angle α, in accordance with the bound given above, and to 32 units at a reflex vertex, whereas a pocket within the same distance of a cushion is moved onto it, with a capture radius of 28 units and a centre displaced by 6 units outwards, as for the side pockets of the classic table, and any other pocket lies in the interior of the table with a capture radius of 30 units. The starting positions are found by scanning the interior on a lattice of 10 units, in order of decreasing distance from the cushions, for a point at which the balls can be packed in the triangle of the standard rack in one of twelve orientations spaced by 30°. If the triangle fits nowhere, the same search is repeated for seven other close packings of hexagonal rows (3, 4, 4 and 4 balls, three rows of 5, rows of 4, 5, 4 and 2, five rows of 3, rows of 8 and 7, seven pairs and a single ball, and a straight line of 15), and if none of these fits either, the balls are added one at a time at the lattice point nearest to the centroid of those already placed that lies at least 2R + 1.5 from each of them, which fills a table of any shape that has room for them. In every case the ball nearest to the centre of the group becomes the eight ball, and the cue ball is placed at the lattice point that is distant from the group, has a clear line of sight to it and lies behind its axis.

Aiming help

The aiming help has three settings, which the Aim button cycles. With Off only the cue is drawn. With Line the shot ray, turned by the squirt angle of equation (10) for the selected hit point, is intersected with discs of radius 2R centred on the other balls. With f the vector from the centre of an object ball to the cue ball and d′ the unit direction of the shot, the first contact lies at

t = −b − √(b2 − c), b = f · d′, c = |f|2 − 4R2,(18)

provided that b2 − c ≥ 0 and t > 0. The distance to the cushions is found by stepping along the ray in increments of 3 units until the ball centre comes within R of a segment and then bisecting the last step ten times, and the smaller of this distance and the smallest t over all balls fixes where the ghost ball is drawn, together with the direction in which the object ball will leave. With Path the game does not use this construction but runs the shot itself on a copy of the table, with the selected power and hit point and with the same equations as the real shot, until every ball has stopped, for at most 6000 steps, and draws the centres of the balls every sixth step, the cue ball in solid white and the others in fainter white, a red dot marking a ball that is potted and a dashed ring the position of the cue ball at its first contact, all in white except the red dots. The prediction contains swerve, throw, draw and cushion effects. The displayed game advances the balls in fixed steps of exactly 1/ s, whatever the frame rate, and the shot contains no random element, so that the path drawn is the path the balls then follow, provided that the hit point, the power and the direction are not changed between the preview and the release.

Rotation of the balls

The rotation of the balls follows the angular velocity. Each ball carries an orientation matrix Q, and in a time dt it is multiplied by a rotation through

θ = |ω| dt about â = ω/|ω|,(19)

applied through Rodrigues' formula Q ← [I cos θ + sin θ [â]× + (1 − cos θ) ââT] Q. For a rolling ball ω = (−vy, vx, 0)/R, which is the condition for rolling without slipping, but a ball that slides, spins about a vertical axis or has just been struck with draw turns differently from its displacement, and the picture shows that. To draw a ball, each pixel of its disc at (u, v) is lifted to the sphere point p = (u, v, √(1 − u2 − v2)) and transformed into the body frame,

q = QT p, stripe if |qy| < ,(20)

so that the pixel is white where it falls on the band of a striped ball and takes the ball colour elsewhere. The number discs sit at ±z in the body frame and are drawn with the affine map given by the first two columns of Q, which makes them foreshorten as they turn away from the viewer. The orientation is a result of the motion and does not feed back into it.

The computer opponent

The computer opponent uses the same engine. For each candidate shot, defined by an angle, a speed obtained from equation (8) for a chosen travel distance and, for one of the styles, a hit point, the position is simulated forward and scored, with its own potted balls counting in its favour, fouls and potted balls of the opponent counting against it, and a further term rewarding a cue ball left with a clear line to the next ball and, when no ball is potted, penalising a cue ball that leaves the opponent such a line. Potting lines that would leave the table outline, as happens across the notch of the cross, are discarded. The selected shot is then perturbed by a random angular error of about °, °, ° and ° at the Easy, Medium, Hard and Pro levels respectively, together with a relative power error, so that the levels differ in how well the shot is chosen (no search at Easy, only simulated pots at Medium, and a search over the best and potting lines at Hard and Pro) as well as in how accurately it is executed. In headless games on the classic table, with six games per pairing and the players alternating the break, Pro beat Easy six games to none, Medium five to one and Hard three to three, Hard beat Medium six to none and Medium beat Easy six to none, which orders the levels as labelled except that Pro and Hard are not separated by so small a sample.

The style changes the weights of the score and the speeds that are tried from the Medium level upwards, whereas at the Easy level it alters only the angular error. The Balanced style uses the weights above. The Aggressive style raises the reward for a potted ball by 40 per cent, halves the weight of the position term, lowers the penalty of a foul to 60 per cent, tries speeds up to 1.9 times the estimate and adds 30 per cent to the angular error. The Safe style raises the weight of the position from 1 to 1.8 and that of the opponent's chances from 1.4 to 2.4, raises the foul penalty by half, tries speeds down to 0.6 of the estimate and reduces the angular error by 15 per cent. The Technical style keeps the Balanced weights apart from a position weight of 1.3 and, for each potting line, also tries follow, draw and side spin of 0.6 and 0.5 of the largest offset, aiming at the angle that cancels the squirt of equation (10), so that it is the only style that uses the hit point. In eight games each against the Balanced style at the Pro level the Aggressive style won four, the Safe style five and the Technical style six, differences that are within what chance alone would give for such a sample.

Limits of the model

The model still omits the vertical motion of the ball, so that there are no jump shots, no massé shots with a steep cue (the elevation is fixed at °) and no lift at the cushion, the dependence of the friction coefficients on speed and humidity, the change of the cloth with wear and the possibility of a miscue at a large offset, which is avoided by limiting the offset. The numerical factors κ, q, μB, μW, ζ and VJ were tuned for a plausible feel, whilst as, μB and the restitution coefficients were set near the values that Alciatore (technical proofs TP A.6, TP B.2 and TP B.6, billiards.colostate.edu) gives as typical for pool: a ball–cloth sliding friction of 0.2, which for a ball of 57 mm diameter (2R = 30 units, so that one unit is 1.9 mm) is as = units per second squared, a ball–ball friction of 0.06, a ball–ball restitution of 0.94 and a ball–cushion restitution of about 0.7 for a rolling ball. The pivoting decay of the side spin was compared with his measured deceleration of about 10 rad s−2 only in order of magnitude, and nothing here has been measured on a real table.
